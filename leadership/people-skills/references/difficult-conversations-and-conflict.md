# Difficult conversations and conflict

Assumes the base frameworks in SKILL.md (Carnegie, OCEAN, Radical Candor) are already applied.

## Before raising it

- Separate the observed behavior from the story you've built about intent - "they didn't reply for two days" is an observation; "they're avoiding me" is a story. Name both to the user explicitly and check which one they're about to act on, since acting on the unverified story is the most common way a hard conversation goes wrong before it even starts.
- Decide the actual goal of the conversation before starting it: repair the relationship, change a specific behavior, or set a boundary. These require different openings, and conflating them is why a lot of conflict conversations end with everyone agreeing on nothing concrete.

## In the conversation

- Open with the observation and the impact, not the interpretation: "when X happened, Y resulted" beats "you don't respect my time." The former is checkable and hard to argue with; the latter is a character claim and invites a defense of character, not a discussion of behavior.
- Ask before telling: "what's your read on what happened" surfaces information that changes the right response more often than it doesn't - the same behavior can come from very different causes (capacity, priority conflict, genuine disagreement, a misunderstanding you don't have yet).
- Naming the Radical Candor quadrant out loud to yourself mid-conversation is a legitimate live check: if you notice you're avoiding the direct sentence because it feels unkind, that's Ruinous Empathy in the moment, not evidence the conversation should stop.

## De-escalation when it's already heated

- Slow down the pace, not the substance - a slower, calmer restatement of the actual disagreement de-escalates; abandoning the substance to keep the peace is Ruinous Empathy again, just under time pressure.
- If Agreeableness or Neuroticism reads high on the other side, expect withdrawal rather than escalation as the stress response - don't mistake quiet for resolved; it often means "shutting down," not "agreeing."
- A conversation that cannot resolve in the room needs an explicit, scheduled follow-up with a stated topic - "let's revisit this" without a time and topic reliably doesn't happen.
